Understanding Transformers

Positional Encoding, Three Ways

The same idea explained as a technical walkthrough, a story, and a visual map — pick your tab.

1
The Problem

Attention is order-blind

Self-attention computes a weighted sum over all tokens using queries, keys and values: S = softmax(QKᵀ / √d_k), then output = S·V. Nothing in that computation depends on where a token sits in the sequence — swap two words and the attention math produces the same result, just relabeled.

That's fine for a "bag of words," but language depends on order. "John likes coffee, he went to the cafe" and a scrambled version of it are not the same sentence. The model needs a signal that tells it where each token is, not just what it is.

Why it matters: without positional information, "the dog bit the man" and "the man bit the dog" would look identical to attention.
2
The Classic Fix

Add a positional vector to the token embedding

The original Transformer's answer: give every position its own vector, and add it directly to the token's embedding before anything else happens. input = token embedding + positional embedding. The combined vector then carries both "what" and "where" into the rest of the network.

The positional vectors themselves come from sine and cosine functions at different frequencies:

PE(pos, 2i) = sin(pos / n^(2i/d))
PE(pos, 2i+1) = cos(pos / n^(2i/d))

Here pos is the token's position in the sequence, i indexes the embedding dimension pair, d is the total embedding dimension, and n is a large constant (typically 10,000). Even dimensions get a sine, odd dimensions get a cosine, each at a different frequency.

Why sine and cosine: they're bounded, smooth, and — crucially — the encoding for one position can be expressed as a linear function of any other, giving the model an easy way to learn relative offsets.
3
Worked Example

What the numbers actually look like

For a toy case with d=4, n=100, and the sentence "I am a robot," each row is a position and each column is computed from the formula above. Position 0 always starts at sin(0)=0, cos(0)=1. As position increases, the low-frequency dimensions change slowly, while high-frequency dimensions oscillate faster.

That mix of frequencies is the trick: some dimensions distinguish neighboring positions, others distinguish far-apart ones, so the model gets a fine-grained and coarse-grained sense of distance in a single vector.

Why it generalizes: because it's a formula and not a lookup table, the model can compute encodings for sequence lengths longer than anything seen in training.
4
The Limitation

Additive encodings blur into the residual stream

Adding position to content mixes the two signals together permanently at the input. As that combined vector travels through many layers of attention and feed-forward blocks, the clean separation between "what" and "where" degrades — and the relative-position math the sinusoids were designed to support turns out weaker in practice, especially at long distances.

This is the gap that motivated newer approaches — encode position where attention actually uses it: inside the query/key dot product, not before it.
5
The Modern Fix

RoPE: rotate instead of add

Rotary Positional Encoding takes a different approach entirely: instead of adding a position vector to the embedding, it rotates the query and key vectors by an angle proportional to their position, treating pairs of dimensions as points on a 2D plane.

A query at position u gets rotated by angle uθ; a key at position u+k gets rotated by (u+k)θ. When the attention dot product QKᵀ is computed, the rotations combine such that the result depends only on the relative distance k — not on absolute positions.

Why this is better: relative position is encoded directly into the attention score itself, it decays naturally with distance, and it extrapolates to longer sequences far more gracefully than additive sinusoidal encoding.
6
Where It Lives

Back to the architecture diagram

In the original Transformer diagram, "Positional Encoding" is one small addition block feeding into the encoder and decoder stacks — easy to skim past. RoPE relocates that job: instead of a one-time addition at the bottom, position gets baked into every attention layer's Q and K vectors, every time attention is computed.

That's the arc: attention has no order → add sinusoidal position vectors → limitations show up at scale and long context → rotate Q/K instead of adding, so relative position lives inside attention itself. RoPE is what most modern large language models use today.

I. The Room With No Order

Picture a room full of words, each one glowing with meaning. John stands near likes, who stands near coffee. Then, across the room, He stands near went and cafe. Every word can see every other word perfectly — that's the gift of attention. Each one reaches out, measures how relevant every other word is, and draws in what it needs.

But there's a problem. The room has no floor markings. No first place, no last place. If you shuffled every word into a different order, the room would look exactly the same to them. "The dog bit the man" and "the man bit the dog" would feel identical — same words, same glow, same connections. Meaning was scrambling itself, and nobody in the room could tell.

The lesson: attention alone has no memory of sequence — it treats a sentence like a bag of words spilled onto a table.
✦ ✦ ✦

II. The Clockmaker's Gift

So a clockmaker arrived, and gave every word a small wristwatch before it entered the room. Watch number 0 for the first word, watch number 1 for the second, and so on — each watch ticking at its own private mix of speeds, fast hands and slow hands sharing the same face. A word's watch didn't say anything about meaning; it only ever whispered "you are here, this many steps from the start."

The words wore their watches stitched onto their sleeves, fused into their very glow, so that "position" and "meaning" became inseparable the moment they walked in. Now, even scrambled, each word carried proof of where it belonged.

The lesson: sinusoidal positional encoding adds a fixed, formula-built signal — built from sine and cosine waves of different frequencies — directly onto each word's embedding, so position becomes part of its identity before attention ever begins.
✦ ✦ ✦

III. The Watches Grew Faint

The system worked — for a while. But the room was tall, with many floors, and as each word climbed from floor to floor, layer after layer, its watch got jostled and blended with everyone else's glow. By the top floor, the ticking was still there, technically, but faint — muddled into the meaning it was fused to. Words far apart from each other in the room struggled to remember, precisely, how far apart they truly were.

The lesson: because the positional signal is added once and then carried passively through many layers, it degrades — long-range relative position becomes harder for the model to recover cleanly.
✦ ✦ ✦

IV. The Dancers Who Rotated Instead

A new idea arrived — not a watch to wear, but a dance to perform. Each word, right at the moment it reached out to measure another (the very instant of query meets key), would spin in place — rotate by an angle that grew with its position. Word 3 turned a little; word 30 turned much further.

Here was the trick: when two dancers compared their spins mid-reach, the maths cancelled out their absolute positions entirely, and left behind only the angle between them — how far apart they were, relatively, regardless of where the dance began. Two words three steps apart always produced the same relative spin, whether they were words 3-and-6 or words 300-and-303.

The lesson: this is RoPE — Rotary Positional Encoding. Instead of adding position to meaning, it rotates the query and key vectors by an angle proportional to position, so relative distance is baked directly into the attention calculation itself, not diluted by the layers above it.
✦ ✦ ✦

V. The Room, Remade

Today's largest rooms — the ones built by the biggest language models — use the dancers, not the watches. Position isn't stitched onto a word's sleeve and hoped to survive the climb; it's re-enacted, fresh, every single time one word reaches for another. The room finally has a floor. Order survived the climb to the top.
